3. Contingency, Marginal, Conditional & Expected Frequency Tables

3A. Contingency Table & Marginal Frequencies

Joint absolute counts nij and joint relative frequencies fij = nij / N, with row marginals ni• and column marginals n•j.

3B. Conditional Frequencies f(F | X = xi)

Calculated as nij / ni•. Under independence, every row matches the bottom Marginal f(F).

3C. Conditional Frequencies f(X | F = fj)

Calculated as nij / n•j. Under independence, every column matches the right Marginal f(X).

3D. Expected Frequencies Under Independence (Eij) & χ² Terms

Expected count Eij = (ni• · n•j) / N, compared against observed Oij, and each cell’s contribution (Oij − Eij)² / Eij summing to χ².

5. Theoretical Concepts, Interpretation & Association vs. Causality

1. Independence in Probability vs. Statistics

In Probability Theory:
Two events A and B are independent (A ⊥ B) if P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B), which for P(B) > 0 is equivalent to P(A | B) = P(A) (knowing B does not alter the probability of A). For two discrete random variables X and F, independence means their joint distribution factorizes into the product of their marginals for every pair (x, f):
P(X = x, F = f) = P(X = x) · P(F = f)  ⇔  P(F = f | X = x) = P(F = f).
Important distinctions: Independence is not mutual exclusivity (disjoint positive-probability events satisfy P(A ∩ B) = 0 ≠ P(A)P(B) and are strongly dependent), and it is stronger than zero covariance (Cov(X, F) = 0 only rules out linear correlation, not nonlinear dependence).

In Statistics:
With a finite sample of N observations, empirical relative frequencies fij = nij / N rarely satisfy fij = fi• · f•j exactly due to random sampling fluctuation. Therefore, we formulate independence as a null hypothesis H0: pij = pi• · p•j for all cells (i, j). Under H0, the expected frequency is Eij = (ni• · n•j) / N, and discrepancies are summarized by Pearson’s statistic:
χ² = ∑i=1r ∑j=1c (Oij − Eij)² / Eij, which follows approximately a χ² distribution with df = (r − 1)(c − 1) degrees of freedom.

2. Interpreting the Results: Independence vs. Dependence

  • When X and F are Statistically Dependent (e.g., Dataset 1): The conditional distributions f(F | X = xi) differ substantially across rows and deviate from the marginal f(F) (for example, Brute-Force attacks are mostly Blocked at 74.3%, whereas Zero-Day exploits are mostly Breached at 65.0%). Large differences |Oij − Eij| produce a high χ² = 70.23 (df = 6, p = 3.67 × 10−13 < α), so we reject H0 and conclude the variables are statistically dependent.
  • When X and F are Consistent with Independence (e.g., Dataset 2): The conditional protocol shares across Rack Zones A, B, and C closely mirror the marginal distribution of F. Observed counts Oij stay close to expected counts Eij, yielding a small χ² = 2.28 (df = 4, p = 0.685 ≥ α). We fail to reject H0: the minor fluctuations are consistent with random sampling variability under independence.

3. Conclusions: Statistical Association vs. Causality

Rejecting H0 establishes statistical association (dependence), which is strictly weaker than causality:

  • Symmetry of the Test: The χ² statistic is symmetric in X and F (χ²(X, F) = χ²(F, X)). An observational contingency table alone cannot tell whether X causes F or F causes X.
  • Confounding Variables (X ← Z → F): As shown in Dataset 3 (Port-Scan Alerts vs. OS Crash), two variables can exhibit strong statistical dependence (χ² = 24.83, p < 10−6) without a direct causal link. Port-scan alerts do not physically crash the operating system; rather, a shared hidden confounder Z (e.g., unpatched public-facing legacy servers) independently increases both port-scan exposure (X) and crash risk (F).
  • From Association to Causality: Proving a causal effect requires isolating interventions (such as randomized controlled experiments or adjusting for all confounders via causal models), whereas contingency-table χ² tests measure purely observational co-occurrence.
