Online estimation of mean and variance, simulation of m hackers across n systems, and empirical analysis of sampling distributions in inferential statistics.

Exercise 1 — Derivation of Welford’s Recurrence Formulas

Naive computation of variance using the textbook formula Var(X) = (1/n)(∑ xi²) − x̄² is numerically unstable on digital computers: subtracting two large, nearly equal floating-point numbers causes catastrophic cancellation. Welford's algorithm solves this by updating the sum of squared differences SSk = M2,k in a numerically stable, single-pass online recurrence.

1. Knuth’s Recurrence Formula for the Mean

Let x̄k be the sample mean of the first k observations:
x̄k = (1/k) ∑i=1k xi = (1/k) [(∑i=1k−1 xi) + xk]
Since ∑i=1k−1 xi = (k − 1) x̄k−1:
x̄k = [(k − 1) x̄k−1 + xk] / k = x̄k−1 + (xk − x̄k−1) / k

2. Derivation of Welford’s Recurrence for the Sum of Squares (SSk = M2,k)

Define the sum of squared differences from the current mean as:
M2,k = ∑i=1k (xi − x̄k)²

Express each difference relative to the previous mean x̄k−1 using the step offset δk = x̄k − x̄k−1 = (xk − x̄k−1) / k:
xi − x̄k = (xi − x̄k−1) − δk

Splitting the sum into the first k − 1 terms plus the new k-th term:
M2,k = ∑i=1k−1 [(xi − x̄k−1) − δk]² + (xk − x̄k)²
  = ∑i=1k−1 (xi − x̄k−1)² − 2δk ∑i=1k−1 (xi − x̄k−1) + (k − 1)δk² + (xk − x̄k)²

By definition of the mean, the middle sum vanishes identically: ∑i=1k−1 (xi − x̄k−1) = 0. Thus:
M2,k = M2,k−1 + (k − 1)δk² + (xk − x̄k)²

Notice that xk − x̄k = xk − (x̄k−1 + δk) = (xk − x̄k−1) − δk = (k − 1)δk.
Factoring the remaining terms gives:
(k − 1)δk² + [(k − 1)δk]² = (k − 1)δk [δk + (k − 1)δk] = (k − 1)δk [k δk]
Since k δk = xk − x̄k−1 and (k − 1)δk = xk − x̄k:

∴ M2,k = M2,k−1 + (xk − x̄k−1)(xk − x̄k)

3. The 3-Line Core Implementation

The complete streaming logic updates in exactly 3 lines of code without storing any past points:

// Given previous state: count k, mean, M2 (sum of squares)
// Process new incoming observation x:
const delta = x - mean;
mean += delta / k;
M2 += delta * (x - mean);

// Unbiased sample variance with Bessel's correction:
const sampleVariance = k > 1 ? M2 / (k - 1) : 0;

Inferential Statistics & Cybersecurity Discussion

1. What Conclusions Can You Draw from These Comparisons?

Across the m hackers, the grand average of sample means (x̄̄) coincides with the theoretical mean μ, and the grand average of sample variances (s̄²) coincides with the theoretical population variance σ².
Mathematically, this confirms that both statistics are unbiased estimators:
• E[X̄] = μ
• E[S²] = σ² (guaranteed by Bessel's correction factor 1 / (n − 1) inside Welford's formula).

2. Why Are These Results Fundamental in Inferential Statistics?

In real-world security audits, an analyst never knows the ground-truth distribution parameters (μ, σ²) of a hacker collective or enterprise defense. Inferential statistics allows us to estimate these global population parameters from a finite audit sample of n penetration events. Because Welford's sample variance is unbiased, security analysts can reliably construct Confidence Intervals and test hypotheses regarding defense posture without systematic under- or over-estimation.

3. How Does System Size (n) Affect Variability?

Adjusting the slider for n visually demonstrates the Law of Large Numbers on both sampling distributions:

  • Variability of Sampling Means: The variance of the sample mean is given by Var(X̄) = σ² / n, corresponding to a Standard Error (SE) of SE = σ / √n. When n = 8, individual hacker scores fluctuate widely between 3.5 and 8.5. When n = 200, the distribution contracts into a sharp spike tightly clustered around μ = 6.0.
  • Variability of Sampling Variances: The variance of the sample variance scales asymptotically as Var(S²) ≈ [2σ4 + (κ − 3)σ4] / n (where κ is kurtosis). Increasing n eliminates estimation noise in variance, preventing security auditors from misinterpreting random penetration variance as an operational shift in adversary capabilities.
